Ford's walls are too small

A premier who understands the stakes better than most is still thinking one province at a time.

Doug Ford has spent the better part of a year sounding the alarm about Canada's exposure — tariffs, trade dependency, an economy leaning on a single customer. He's not wrong. He's just aiming the fix at the wrong scale.

Ontario's response to a continental problem has been a provincial one: buy local, retaliate locally, protect what's inside the border. It plays well. It also misses the actual opportunity, which was never about defending Ontario from the United States — it was about building something with Mexico and the U.S. that none of the three could build alone.

The wall in the wrong place

Every producer in this country already knows the real barrier isn't at the 49th parallel. It's between provinces. Trucking rules, professional licensing, alcohol distribution, procurement — a company can sell into Michigan easier than it can sell into Manitoba. That's the wall worth tearing down first, and it's one Ontario could lead on unilaterally tomorrow.

The instinct to protect is understandable. The instinct to protect at the provincial border instead of the continental one is a hundred-billion-dollar mistake.

None of this means the trade fight with Washington isn't real — it is, and Ford has been right to name it plainly when others hedged. But a fortress needs a perimeter, not just a room. Ontario building higher internal walls while Alberta, Quebec, and the Maritimes do the same is not strength. It's three ways of losing slower.

What the walls should actually be for

  • Free interprovincial trade, treated as a national security question, not a jurisdictional one.
  • Energy corridors that reach tidewater without a decade of court challenges.
  • A shared posture with Mexico that stops treating nearshoring as a U.S.-only story.

Ford has the instincts. He's also got the biggest microphone of any premier willing to use it on this subject. The question isn't whether he understands the threat — he does. It's whether the walls he's building are big enough to matter.
